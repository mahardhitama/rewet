## Concepts

### 3R framework

- BRG oversees restoration with a "3Rs" approach. The 3Rs are rewetting by canal blocking and backfilling, revegetation, and revitalisation of livelihoods (W20 page 818).
- The 2016 government commitment was to restore over 2 million hectares of tropical peatland by 2020 (W20 page 818).
- BRG was mandated to restore 2.6 million hectares in seven provinces by 2020. It had restored 45% of that target by December 2020 (TC20). W20 gives 2 million hectares. The two sources do not agree. Do not show a target in hectares.
- BRGM counted these results for 2016 to 2020: 13,869 boreholes, 6,631 canal blocks, 324 backfilled canals, 1,187 hectares of revegetation and 801 revitalisation packages (MB22).

### Canals and drainage

- The Mega Rice Project dug 187 km of main canal. It then dug 771 km of main canals, 973 km of secondary canals and 900 km of tertiary canals in Block A alone (R14 page 3).
- Main canals often cut through the centre of peat domes. This causes excessive drainage, subsidence, irreversible drying, habitat loss and a higher fire risk (R14 page 3).
- Illegal loggers dug small canals to move timber. These canals sped up the drainage of the peat forest (R14 page 3).
- Water-table depth next to a canal is 0.40 to 0.50 m deeper than 1,000 m away (R14 page 8). Table 4 (R14 page 25) has the values by distance.
- Illegal loggers dug timber canals 1 to 2 m wide and 0.3 to 1.3 m deep in the Sebangau peat (H18 page 4).
- The main canals of Blok A are about 8 m wide, over 5 m deep when dug, and 2,600 m apart (KFCP14 page 9). See `canal-dimensions.md`.

### The 0.4 m rule

- Article 23(3)(a) of PP 57/2016 says a peat ecosystem with a cultivation function is damaged when the water table is more than 0.4 m below the peat surface at the compliance point. The text reads "muka air tanah di lahan Gambut lebih dari 0,4 (nol koma empat) meter di bawah permukaan Gambut pada titik penaatan" (PP57 page 11).
- Article 23(3)(b) adds a second test: exposed pyrite or quartz sediment under the peat (PP57 page 11).
- The 0.4 m test applies to the cultivation function only. A protected peat ecosystem is damaged when it has artificial drainage, exposed sediment or less land cover (PP57 page 11, Article 23(2)).
- A "compliance point" (titik penaatan) is a place set for water-table monitoring. Its position depends on land traits, topography, water zones, canals and water structures (PP57 page 11, Article 23(4) and (5); elucidation page 5 for the definition).
- R14 gives the same number for fire risk: fire risk rises when the water table falls below 0.40 m (R14 page 2).

### Block cascades

- Dams raised the average water table along three transects from 1.12 m to 0.37 m below the surface (R14 page 10).
- The water table can still fall below 1 m in the dry season (R14 page 10).
- In the dry season after the dams, the water tables fell again to well below 0.40 m. Water seeped along and under the dams (R14 page 8).
- The head difference over a dam must stay under 0.5 m. A cascade of many robust dams, each with a bypass, is therefore necessary (R14 page 11).
- Some local people destroyed canal blocks because they did not know the purpose of the blocks (W20 page 824).

### Fire

- Fire risk rises if the water table falls below 0.40 m. This adds to the CO2 emission from the peat (R14 page 2).
- Fires burned 1.7 million hectares of forest and land in Indonesia to October 2015. The largest fires were on peat, mainly in South Sumatra and Central Kalimantan (PP57 elucidation page 1, "Umum").
- A wildfire on drained tropical peat burns 353 t of dry matter per hectare (95% interval 170 to 536) (IPCC13 Table 2.6, page 2.40).
- The 2015 peat fires contributed to over 100,000 deaths, by estimate (W20 page 818, citing Koplitz et al. 2016).
- Peat and forest fires have a major negative effect on biodiversity in Kalimantan (see "Fire and wildlife" in `peat-swamp-wildlife.md`, C19 page 8).
- Village fire groups ("masyarakat peduli api") are part of the fire prevention duty in PP 57/2016 (PP57 page 10, Article 22A(4)(c)).
- The Fire Care Community and Fire Care Village programmes are community-based fire prevention programmes (TC20).

### Subsidence and carbon

- Subsidence rate in cm per year equals 0.1 times the water-table depth in cm (R14 page 2).
- In drained peat, CO2 emission and subsidence rise sharply as the water table falls (R14 page 2).
- Drained tropical oil-palm plantation on peat emits 11 t CO2-C per hectare per year (95% interval 5.6 to 17) (IPCC13 Table 2.1, page 2.12).
- Rewetted tropical organic soil has an emission factor of 0 t CO2-C per hectare per year. The condition is that saturated conditions stop the oxidation of decayed organic material (IPCC13 Table 3.1, page 3.12).
- The game shows carbon as t C. These IPCC13 values are on-site CO2-C only. DOC, CH4 and N2O are separate.

### Revegetation

- BRG revegetation means planting endemic and adaptive trees (TC20).
- On deforested and burned peat in Indonesia, rewetting alone is not enough. Afforestation is also necessary. Without swamp forest, high water levels cannot stay and subsidence continues (FAO16 page 3).
- H18 recommends the replanting of burned areas (H18 page 8, recommendation 1).
- Dams that keep the dry-season water table closer to the peat surface can be expected to help vegetation regrow. Subsidence data show that organic matter still builds up in natural forest, but not in degraded peat. The monitoring period is too short for firm conclusions (R14 page 10).

### Paludiculture

- Paludiculture is the cultivation of biomass in wet conditions. It makes biomass from wet and rewetted peat and keeps the peat body (FAO16 page 2).
- Experts at the 2016 workshop named these species: nipa, sago, jelutong (*Dyera polyphylla*), tengkawang nut trees (*Shorea* spp.) and rattan (*Calamus* spp.) (FAO16 page 3).
- In Indonesia, paludiculture should focus on tree species from peat swamp forests (FAO16 page 3).
- Purun, a herbaceous plant for bioenergy, can grow in flooded areas that burned before (FAO16 page 3).
- Tropical sago on shallow-drained peat (water table less than 0.3 m deep) has a CO2-C emission factor of 1.5 t per hectare per year (95% interval -2.3 to 5.4) (IPCC13 Table 2.1, page 2.12).
- Limit: respondents said that no known crop gives a household income and also prevents peat degradation. Until markets and prices match supply and demand, the potential of paludiculture appears limited (W20 page 823).

### Livelihoods

- Revitalisation means alternative community livelihoods (TC20).
- BRGM recorded 801 revitalisation packages for 2016 to 2020, and 279 livelihood revitalisation units in 2021 (MB22).
- The Desa Peduli Gambut (Peatland Care Village) programme funds villages around peat that BRGM restores. The villages take part in making the peat resistant to fire (P24 page 6).
- Many households in Indonesia depend on peatland for market-based agriculture. This work helped to reduce poverty (W20 page 823).
- Fishing is a peatland livelihood. It is particularly common in Kalimantan. There is evidence that it is becoming unsustainable because of human pressure (W20 page 823).
- Fish farming in the traditional "beje" system can use existing canals, low peat areas and fire-prevention water reservoirs. The 2016 workshop suggested this as a livelihood option (FAO16 page 3).
- Oil palm raised the income of some smallholders in Sumatra. In Kalimantan its benefit is less clear (W20 page 823).

### Dry and wet seasons

- In the dry season, the water tables are 0.30 m deeper on average than in the rainy season (R14 page 8).
- Water-table depth at 10 m, 100 m, 400 m and 1,000 m from Kalampangan Canal: dry season 0.88, 0.54, 0.35 and 0.33 m; rainy season 0.50, 0.13, 0.05 and 0.06 m (R14 Table 4, page 25).
- In the dry season after dam construction, the water tables fell again to well below 0.40 m (R14 page 8).

### Peat fire emissions

- A wildfire on drained tropical peat burns 353 t of dry matter per hectare (IPCC13 Table 2.6, page 2.40).
- A tropical organic-soil fire gives 464 g CO2-C, 210 g CO and 21 g CH4 per kg of dry matter burnt (IPCC13 Table 2.7, page 2.41).
- Calculation: 353 t d.m. per hectare × 0.464 = 163.8 t C per hectare, about 164 t C. Divided by 11 t C per hectare per year for drained oil palm (IPCC13 Table 2.1), that is 14.9 years, about 15 years.

### Land use on peat

- Tier 1 CO2 emission factors for drained tropical organic soils, in t CO2-C per hectare per year: short-rotation plantation (for example acacia) 20, cropland 14, oil palm 11, paddy rice 9.4, shallow-drained plantation under 0.3 m (for example sago palm) 1.5 (IPCC13 Table 2.1, pages 2.12 and 2.13).
- Rewetted tropical organic soil: 0 (IPCC13 Table 3.1, page 3.12).

### Carbon and CO2

- The conversion from C to CO2 uses the ratio of molecular weights, 44/12 (IPCC06 Volume 4, Section 2.2.3, page 2.11). See `peat-hydrology-and-emissions.md`.
- Calculation: 1 t C × 44/12 = 3.67 t CO2.

### Peat stores carbon

- Peat is "the surface layer of soil, consisting mostly of partially decomposed vegetation, with an organic content of at least 65% in a minimum thickness of 30 cm" (PAGE11 page 5).
- "Published values of carbon content of Southeast Asian peats range from 41.6% … to 62.0%", as a fraction of the dry weight. PAGE11 uses 56±3% (PAGE11 pages 16 and 17).
- "A single country, Indonesia, holds the largest share (57.4 Gt, 65%)" of the tropical peat carbon pool (PAGE11 abstract, page 2). The manuscript gives 88.5 Gt (range 81.5 to 91.8) for all tropical peat. A search snippet gives 88.6 Gt for the published paper, which we could not open, so the journal does not show the total.
- "Draining inland organic soils lowers the water table and increases the oxygen content of the soil, thus increasing CO2 emissions" (IPCC13 Chapter 1, Box 1.1, page 1.9).
- The same box: rewetting "decreases CO2 emissions … and increases CH4 emissions compared to the drained state" (IPCC13 page 1.9). The journal does not show CH4 yet.
- Scope: PAGE11 counts the carbon in the peat soil only.

### Baseline and project

- Net emission reductions of a Wetlands Restoration and Conservation (WRC) project: `NER_WRC = GHG_BSL-WRC − GHG_WPS-WRC − GHG_LK-WRC`, the baseline scenario minus the project scenario minus leakage, up to year t*, in t CO2e (VM0007 Section 8.4.3, Equation 5, page 33).
- The baseline "must be consistent with the description of the conditions prior to the project start date" (VM0007 Section 6.1, page 25).
- Rewetting of Drained Peatland (RDP) is a subcategory of the WRC project category (VM0007 pages 12 and 16, footnote 13).
- Leakage: "Net changes in anthropogenic emissions by GHG sources that occur outside the project or program boundary, but are attributable to the project or program" (VCSDEF page 14).
- GHG emission reduction: a reduction "net of associated project and leakage emissions" (VCSDEF page 10).
